Article summaries

  1. 1

    Finance at War: How Conflict Redefines the Global Economy

    Finance is increasingly becoming a frontline of conflict rather than a neutral infrastructure. It highlights how sovereign reserves, payment systems and energy flows are being weaponized: for example, more than $300 billion of one country’s central bank reserves were frozen in 2022, and over 11,000 sanctions were applied in a year.

    Identifying three dynamics: (1) the financial battlefield (reserves, payment rails, currencies); (2) the refuges of finance (gold binge, crypto donations in conflict zones) showing inequality in resilience; (3) the future of finance a shift toward multiple currency blocs, central‑bank digital currencies (CBDCs) and fragmentation of the global system. The conclusion: the governance of finance may be as important to global stability as military power.

  2. 2

    Turbulent times: geopolitical risk and its impact on euro area financial stability

    Prepared by the European Central Bank (ECB), this article examines how elevated geopolitical risk affects banks and non‑banks in the euro‑area. It shows that while adverse geopolitical events alone have historically not triggered systemic crises, they can act as triggers for distress when combined with other vulnerabilities.

    Key channels of impact include: increased funding costs and liquidity risk for weaker banks; higher risk premia on bank debt; and knock‑on effects on credit and asset quality. The article underscores the need for vigilance from both institutions and regulators in the face of a fragmented, volatile global environment.

  3. 3

    How Rising Geopolitical Risks Weigh on Asset Prices

    From the International Monetary Fund (IMF) blog, this article outlines how heightened geopolitical tensions reduce investor confidence, raise sovereign borrowing costs and increase tail‑risk for asset markets. It explains that such risks are hard to price due to their unusual, unpredictable nature which means when they materialize, markets can react sharply.

    The piece notes that wars, diplomatic breakdowns or major trade disruptions not only damage growth but also hurt lending, cross‑border investment and market sentiment.
