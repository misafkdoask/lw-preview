Article summaries

  1. 1

    Global bond-market selloff and the return of inflation risk

    Global bond markets are undergoing significant repricing as investors reassess the outlook for inflation, interest rates and government borrowing. Yields have risen sharply across major economies, with Japan's 10-year government bond yield reaching levels not seen in decades.

    Higher energy prices, partly driven by geopolitical tensions in the Middle East, are adding to inflation concerns and making further monetary tightening more likely.

    The consequences extend beyond governments: higher yields raise financing costs for companies and households and could put pressure on equity valuations, particularly highly leveraged sectors such as technology.

    Sourcereuters.com (opens in a new tab)
  2. 2

    U.S. employment, the Federal Reserve and political pressure

    A stronger-than-expected U.S. employment picture is complicating the Federal Reserve's monetary-policy decisions. Robust job creation and inflation that remains above the central bank's target have increased expectations that rates could rise rather than fall.

    At the same time, President Donald Trump is publicly pushing for lower rates, creating an unusual tension between political demands and the Federal Reserve's traditional independence.

    Markets are therefore watching upcoming inflation data closely, since the numbers could determine whether policymakers prioritize controlling prices or supporting economic activity.

    Sourcereuters.com (opens in a new tab)
  3. 3

    Oil, the Strait of Hormuz and global markets

    Financial markets are attempting to balance strong technology-sector performance against renewed geopolitical risks in the Middle East. Escalating tensions involving the U.S. and Iran have pushed crude prices higher, reviving concerns about energy-driven inflation.

    At the same time, strong U.S. employment data has increased expectations of tighter Federal Reserve policy. Asian technology shares nevertheless performed strongly, while investors remain focused on upcoming U.S. inflation figures and expected central-bank decisions in the U.S., Europe and Japan.

    Sourcereuters.com (opens in a new tab)

Across all three pieces, a clear theme emerges:
Geopolitics is no longer a background risk it is actively driving financial markets, fiscal policy, and corporate strategy in real time.
